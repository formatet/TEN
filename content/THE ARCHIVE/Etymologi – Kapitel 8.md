## The Weight of History: Morrison, Heaney, Coetzee

*Part of [[The Etymological Archive]] | Chapter thread: historical guilt as grammatical and syntactic construction*

---

The words in this chapter are heavy. They carry history in their etymology — not metaphorically but literally: every root is a trace of how people previously handled the same questions of guilt, testimony, and memory. That is what the chapter is about.

---

## The Words

**PERIPHERAL**
- Origin: Greek *periphereia* — the circumference, that which runs around the edge (peri = around, pherein = to carry)
- Weight: Not merely "on the edge" — actively kept from the centre by a force that defines where the centre is. Morrison used the concept of her own reading position: her experience was made *peripheral* by the white literary centre.
- Avoid instead: *marginal, minor*
- Write: Write a sentence where PERIPHERAL describes a power relation, not a geographical position.
- Cross-reference: [[Etymologi – Kapitel 5]] COLONIAL — both words describe an effect of the same movement.

**WEIGHT / WEIGHT OF HISTORY**
- Origin: Old English *wegan* — to carry, to weigh (same root as *weigh*, *wagon*, Latin *vehere* = to carry/drive)
- Weight: In Morrison, "weight" is never merely mass. It is what a body carries and cannot put down — history as physical burden, not abstract fact.
- Avoid instead: *burden, heaviness*
- Exercise: Hemingway used *stark* (Chapter 3) — a word without softness. Morrison uses *weight*. What is the difference? Can you construct a sentence where you hear the difference?
- Cross-reference: [[Etymologi – Kapitel 3]] STARK, IMPASSIVE

**NEUTRAL**
- Origin: Latin *neutralis* — of neither gender (*ne* = not, *uter* = one of two). From *neuter* — neither one nor the other.
- Weight: To claim neutrality is always a political choice. Morrison: "language is not neutral" — every sentence is inherited from the world that built it. Neutrality as a claim is one of power's strategies.
- Avoid instead: *objective, unbiased*
- Write: Choose a word from everyday life that claims to be neutral but carries an implicit perspective. Show it in a sentence.

**PASTORAL**
- Origin: Latin *pastor* — shepherd (one who leads a flock to pasture); *pascere* = to feed, to graze
- Weight: Pastoral literature idealises country life as a counterweight to the urban and political. Heaney *is* pastoral — but his pastoral is always underlaid by history and political violence. To call Heaney pastoral without seeing that as a *problem* for him is to have missed half the poetry.
- Avoid instead: *rural, countryside*
- Exercise: Read "Digging" line by line. Where is it *pastoral* in the traditional sense? Where does that idyll end?

**COMPLICIT**
- Origin: Latin *complicitus* — entwined with; *com-* (together) + *plicare* (to fold) — same root as *complicate*, *implicit*, *explicit*
- Weight: To be complicit is to be folded into something. You did not begin it; you benefit from it; you do not stop it. Gordimer's white South African characters are complicit. Coetzee's narrators are complicit. The word does not accuse — it describes a structural position.
- Avoid instead: *guilty, responsible*
- Write: Write a sentence where COMPLICIT describes a structural position rather than a personal failing.

**ELEGY / ELEGIAC**
- Origin: Greek *elegeia* — a lament (see also [[Etymologi – Kapitel 2]])
- Weight — year 3 deepening: In year two, elegy was a form. In year three, it is a burden. Heaney's North is not elegiac *about* the Troubles — it is elegiac *because of* them, because elegy is the only form adequate to losses that cannot be resolved. Morrison's *Beloved* is an elegy that refuses to be finished. The elegiac mode in year three is how literature honours what cannot be undone.
- Cross-reference: [[Etymologi – Kapitel 2]] ELEGY — revisit that entry before this one

**CLINICAL**
- Origin: Greek *klinikos* — of the bed (*klinē* = bed, couch — from *klinein* = to lean)
- Weight: Clinical precision originated at the sickbed: the doctor who observes without flinching. In literature: Coetzee's prose is clinical. It does not console. It observes the wound with the steadiness of someone who has decided to see what is there.
- Avoid instead: *cold, detached*
- Connection: [[Etymologi – Kapitel 1]] DETACHED — clinical is detachment with a medical instrument in hand.

**SCRUTINY**
- Origin: Latin *scrutinium* — careful examination; from *scrutari* = to search, to examine; from *scruta* = rubbish, trash (originally: to sort through rags and refuse)
- Weight: Scrutiny comes from the act of sorting refuse — it implies looking at what others have discarded or ignored. Coetzee's scrutiny turns on South Africa's history, on his own complicity, on the mechanisms of power. Morrison's scrutiny turns on what American language has done with race. Both refuse to look away.
- Avoid instead: *attention, examination*
- Write: Write a sentence where SCRUTINY is directed at something comfortable and familiar.

**INCORRUPTIBLE**
- Origin: Latin *incorruptibilis* — *in-* (not) + *corruptus* (broken, spoiled, rotted) — from *corrumpere* = to break to pieces, to destroy (*cor-* intensive + *rumpere* = to break)
- Weight: That which cannot be broken or bought. Heaney's attention to the bog bodies is incorruptible — the poem does not flinch to serve a political position. Morrison's prose is incorruptible in its refusal to let the reader off the historical hook.
- Avoid instead: *honest, reliable*
- Connection: [[Etymologi – Kapitel 4]] DELIBERATE — both describe a discipline of refusal.
