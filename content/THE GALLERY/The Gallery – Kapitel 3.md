## Sinclair Lewis (1885–1951), United States

Sinclair Lewis grew up in a small town in Minnesota. He did not like it. He spent the rest of his life writing about people trapped in small towns who also did not like them but could not leave.

His prose is **vernacular** — it sounds like American speech, flat and precise and slightly cruel. His characters say exactly the wrong thing and do not notice. They belong to clubs and attend church and buy the right furniture and are **impassive** about everything that matters.

*Main Street* and *Babbitt* are his best-known novels. They are comedies without a punchline. The joke is the life inside them.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1930. He was the first American to win it. He said the prize was given to him because his books were useful criticisms of America. He was probably right.

## Ernest Hemingway (1899–1961), United States

Ernest Hemingway was a journalist before he was a novelist. He learned to cut. He learned that the reader fills in what the writer leaves out — and fills it in with something more powerful than anything the writer could have put there.

His sentences are **terse**. They do not explain. A man and a woman sit at a table. They order drinks. They talk about something that is not what they are talking about. You know what they are talking about. He never says it.

This is the iceberg theory: the story works because of what is below the surface. The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. He learned it watching the bullfights in Spain and the wars in Italy and the losses he did not write about.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1954. The prize committee called his style "**laconic**." He would have approved of the word.

## William Faulkner (1897–1962), United States

William Faulkner was born in Mississippi. He lived most of his life there. He wrote about the American South the way a geologist writes about rock: as a record of enormous pressure applied over a very long time.

His sentences do not end. They fold back on themselves, accumulate, spiral. A single Faulkner sentence can contain a life, a family, a century of shame. He was not **laconic**. He was the opposite: he believed that only by circling a thing from every angle could you begin to see its true shape.

His characters carry their history in their blood and their grammar. The way Benjy Compson speaks in *The Sound and the Fury* is not a style choice. It is what trauma does to time.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1949. In his acceptance speech, he said a writer must never be afraid to write about courage, honour, hope, pride, compassion, pity, sacrifice. He said these are the old universal truths. He spent his life proving they were not simple.
