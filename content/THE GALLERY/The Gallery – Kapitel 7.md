## T.S. Eliot (1888–1965), United States/United Kingdom

Thomas Stearns Eliot was born in St. Louis, Missouri, educated at Harvard and Oxford, and settled in London in 1914, where he remained for the rest of his life — a permanently displaced American who became the most influential English-language poet of the twentieth century, which was itself a kind of **peripheral** becoming central.

*The Waste Land* (1922) is a poem built from fragments: quotations in six languages, half-finished narratives, voices without bodies, scenes that begin and do not end. It does not explain its own structure. It does not offer the reader a position of comfort or authority. It insists that **fragmentation** is not a failure of form but a form — the form appropriate to a civilisation that had just sent ten million men to die in the mud of Belgium and France and was now trying to pretend it knew what it was.

His Nobel Prize in 1948 was awarded for "outstanding, pioneer contribution to present-day poetry." He is introduced briefly in Chapter Two of this course as the limit of what Year One can hold. He belongs here, in the chapter about what language does when it stops pretending to be whole.

## Doris Lessing (1919–2013), United Kingdom/Zimbabwe

Doris Lessing grew up in Southern Rhodesia, the daughter of a farmer who had been wounded in the First World War and had never entirely returned from it. She left Africa at thirty, arrived in London with her third child and the manuscript of her first novel, and spent the next six decades writing with a fury that did not diminish.

*The Golden Notebook* — her central work — is a novel about a woman who cannot hold her life together in a single narrative and so builds four separate notebooks: one for politics, one for fiction, one for daily reality, one for emotion. None of them is sufficient. The **fragmentation** is not a formal experiment; it is a diagnosis of what it means to be a certain kind of woman in a world that has not been designed to hold that kind of complexity.

Lessing received the Nobel Prize in 2007, at eighty-seven. She heard the news on her doorstep and said, flatly: "Oh Christ." It is a sentence worth examining.

## Samuel Beckett (1906–1989), Ireland/France

Samuel Beckett was an Irishman who chose to write in French so that he would be forced to write without style. He wanted language reduced to its bones — to what remained when everything decorative had been stripped away — and he found that what remained was enough to hold the whole of human experience, or at least its most honest residue.

*Waiting for Godot* contains two men, a tree, and the structure of all waiting. Vladimir and Estragon cannot leave, cannot stop talking, cannot make the talking mean anything conclusive, and cannot stop trying. The comedy is not incidental to the **weight** of what they carry. It is how the weight becomes bearable.

Beckett said: *"Ever tried. Ever failed. No matter. Try again. Fail again. Fail better."* This is not optimism. It is something more **incorruptible** than optimism: a refusal to pretend that success and failure are the only two options.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1969 "for his writing, which — in new forms for the novel and drama — in the destitution of modern man acquires its elevation."

## Harold Pinter (1930–2008), United Kingdom

Harold Pinter grew up in the East End of London, the son of a Jewish tailor, and spent his childhood understanding that what people say and what they mean are not reliably the same thing, and that the gap between them is where power lives.

His plays are built from that gap. A man is questioned in his room by two strangers. Tea is offered. Pleasantries are exchanged. The menace is **peripheral** at first, then central, then total — but it is never named. The most frightening thing in a Pinter play is rarely what is said. It is what the pause before the sentence contains.

He received the Nobel Prize in 2005. His acceptance speech — "Art, Truth and Politics" — is one of the most direct political statements ever made in a Nobel lecture. He said that political language is designed to hide reality, not describe it, and that the writer's task is to "find the truth" which is "elusive" and "often difficult to locate." He said this in a speech that was itself a model of how to locate it.

## Joseph Brodsky (1940–1996), Soviet Union/United States

Joseph Brodsky was born in Leningrad and expelled from the Soviet Union in 1972, told to leave and take his language with him. He arrived in the United States at thirty-two, was given a position at the University of Michigan, and spent the rest of his life writing Russian poetry and English essays with equal mastery — a bilingual achievement so rare it constitutes its own argument about what language is.

His essays in English are written in a prose that is visibly translated from Russian thought: denser, more formally intricate, more willing to hold a paradox without resolving it than most English prose allows itself to be. He understood exile not as deprivation but as **clarity** — as the condition that strips away the **peripheral** and forces the writer to address only what is essential.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1987 for "an all-embracing authorship, imbued with clarity of thought and poetic intensity." He served as United States Poet Laureate in 1991. He died at fifty-five.
