## Nadine Gordimer (1923–2014), South Africa

Nadine Gordimer grew up white in South Africa — which meant she grew up inside a system designed to protect her from the truth. She spent her life writing her way out of that protection.

Her novels do not offer the reader a comfortable distance. The white characters in her fiction are **complicit**, compromised, ordinary. They are not villains. They are people who have chosen not to look — and Gordimer's prose makes looking **inevitable**.

She understood that **colonial** power does not only deform the colonised. It deforms the coloniser in quieter, harder-to-name ways. Her characters carry that deformation in the way they speak, the parties they attend, the silences they keep at dinner tables.

She received the Nobel Prize in 1991. The apartheid government had banned several of her books. She kept writing.

## Wole Soyinka (b. 1934), Nigeria

Wole Soyinka was born in Abeokuta, western Nigeria, the son of a school headmaster and a market trader known as "the Wild Christian." He studied literature at the University of Leeds, returned to Nigeria, and spent the rest of his life moving between theatre and prison and exile — sometimes rapidly.

He writes in English because Nigeria gave him English as the language of education and power. He uses it to dismantle the colonial self-image that built the schools that taught it. His plays draw on Yoruba myth and ritual as structures that Western drama had no framework to contain, and he does not apologise for the difficulty this creates.

He was imprisoned for two years during the Nigerian civil war for attempting to negotiate peace. He wrote poems in prison on toilet paper with a homemade pen. When the colonial rhetoric of civilisation and order is examined closely enough, it reveals itself as a system that imprisoned a man for trying to stop a war.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1986, the first African to do so. The committee cited "the wide cultural perspective and with poetic overtones fashions the drama of existence."

## Derek Walcott (1930–2017), Saint Lucia

Derek Walcott was born on the small Caribbean island of Saint Lucia, son of a civil servant father who died when Walcott was one year old, and a schoolteacher mother who read Shakespeare aloud to her class. He grew up speaking English in a French Creole world, inside a colonial culture that told him his island was provincial and its history did not matter.

He decided his island was the centre of the world and Homer was a local poet. *Omeros* — his most ambitious work — retells the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* with Saint Lucian fishermen as its heroes, the Caribbean Sea as its wine-dark water, the history of slavery as its wound that will not close. He did not borrow from Homer. He claimed him.

His Nobel Prize in 1992 was awarded for "a poetic oeuvre of great luminosity, sustained by a historical vision, the outcome of a multicultural commitment." He was the first Caribbean writer to win it.

## Abdulrazak Gurnah (b. 1948), Tanzania/United Kingdom

Abdulrazak Gurnah was born in Zanzibar in 1948, a year before the Zanzibar Revolution that would transform his island and eventually force him to leave it. He arrived in England as a refugee in the 1960s, studied literature, and became a professor at the University of Kent. He has lived there ever since.

His novels are written in the **neutral** English of an educated man who learned the language formally and has spent a lifetime understanding what that neutrality conceals. He writes about people who carry two worlds inside them and find neither whole: the world they left, which is now inaccessible, and the world they arrived in, which does not quite let them belong.

The **peripheral** experience — the refugee, the immigrant, the one who arrived from somewhere else — is not peripheral in his work. It is where the twentieth century is most honestly visible.

He received the Nobel Prize in 2021. Many readers had never heard of him. The surprise was a form of proof.

## Patrick White (1912–1990), Australia

Patrick White was born in London to wealthy Australian parents who had come to England for his birth. He was taken back to Australia at six months, educated in England, served in the Second World War, and returned to Australia in 1948, apparently permanently.

He felt he had returned to a country that did not have the literary language to describe itself — flat, dismissive, suspicious of interiority. He spent his life proving this was wrong, by writing novels that went so deep into the Australian landscape and the Australian mind that the landscape became a form of psychology.

*Voss* follows a German explorer attempting to cross the Australian continent in the nineteenth century. The continent defeats him. It does not do so with violence. It does so with its **relentless** refusal to be what a European mind requires it to be.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1973, the only Australian to do so. He used the prize money to establish a literary award for young Australian writers.
