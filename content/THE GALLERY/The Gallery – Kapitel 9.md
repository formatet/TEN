## [[V.S. Naipaul ]](1932–2018), Trinidad/United Kingdom

Vidiadhar Surajprasad Naipaul was born in Trinidad to a family of Indian descent, won a scholarship to Oxford, moved to London, and spent his adult life in the **peripheral** position he understood best: the outside observer, the man who belonged nowhere completely and saw everything more clearly for it.

His prose has what the Nobel committee called "incorruptible scrutiny" — a gaze that applies the same **clinical** precision to colonial societies, post-colonial disorders, and the private failures of individuals, and finds in all of them the same evasions, the same distance between the story a people tell about themselves and the reality **beneath** that story. He was not kind. He was, in the most demanding sense, honest.

*A House for Mr Biswas* is his warmest novel — a comedy of a man's lifelong, partially successful attempt to own a house, which is also the attempt to be a self, to leave a mark, to matter. It is a great book. Naipaul was not always a good man. The two facts coexist in his work without resolution, which is itself a form of honesty.

He received the Nobel Prize in 2001 for "having united perceptive narrative and incorruptible scrutiny."

## [[Kazuo Ishiguro]] (b. 1954), Japan/United Kingdom

Kazuo Ishiguro was born in Nagasaki and brought to England at five. He has lived in Britain ever since. He writes in English about memory, loss, and the slow, precise ways in which human beings construct versions of their own lives that protect them from what they cannot face.

His most celebrated novel, *The Remains of the Day*, is narrated by Stevens, an English butler of the old school, who is driving across England and thinking about his years of service to a lord who turned out to hold fascist sympathies. Stevens does not allow himself to know this. His sentences are **formal**, **oblique**, impeccably correct — and vibrating with everything he has **spent a lifetime refusing to feel**.

This is Ishiguro's central technique: the narrator who does not know what they are telling you. Stevens describes his own life with perfect grammar and total incomprehension of what he is describing, and you read both things simultaneously — the surface account and the subterranean truth — without his permission. It is the hardest effect in fiction to produce and one of the most devastating to experience.

He received the Nobel Prize in 2017 for "novels of great emotional force, who, in his quest to understand our illusory sense of connection with the world, has uncovered the abyss beneath our most ordinary feelings and actions."
