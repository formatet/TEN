## Eugene O'Neill (1888–1953), United States

Eugene O'Neill grew up in a theatre trunk. His father was a famous actor who toured the country in the same play for thirty years, playing the same romantic hero to the same appreciative crowds, and O'Neill spent his childhood watching a man perform a lie about himself with perfect professional conviction. He spent his adult life writing plays that could not be performed in one evening and could not be looked away from.

*Long Day's Journey into Night* takes place in a single day, in a single house, in a family that has been destroying itself for so long that the destruction has become its form of love. The Tyrones drink and blame and forgive and blame again. The irony is not dramatic. It is structural: the very mechanisms by which these people try to hold each other together are the mechanisms by which they come apart.

O'Neill brought a European seriousness to the American stage — Ibsen's moral rigour, Strindberg's psychological violence — and applied it to a country that preferred its theatre cheerful. He received the Nobel Prize in 1936. He did not attend the ceremony. He was ill, or said he was.

## William Golding (1911–1993), United Kingdom

William Golding was a schoolteacher for many years. He knew boys well — their cruelty, their capacity for hierarchy, their ability to persuade themselves that what they are doing is necessary or even noble. *Lord of the Flies* emerged from that knowledge.

A group of British schoolboys are stranded on an uninhabited island during a war. They are **civilised** children. They know the rules. They establish a society. The society collapses. What replaces it is something older and darker and more honest about what the boys were all along.

Golding's great irony is that the novel makes this collapse feel **inevitable** — not because human beings are evil but because civilisation is thinner than we want to believe, and the boys are simply human beings without the structures that usually contain what human beings are. The horror is not exceptional. That is the horror.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1983 for "novels which, with the perspicuity of realistic narrative art and the diversity and universality of myth, illuminate the human condition in the world of today."

## Saul Bellow (1915–2005), Canada/United States

Saul Bellow was born in the Montreal suburb of Lachine to a family of Russian-Jewish immigrants who moved to Chicago when he was nine. He grew up between two worlds: the Yiddish domestic world of his family and the vast, brash, **vernacular** culture of mid-century American cities.

His novels are full of men who are too intelligent for their own happiness. They know too much, feel too much, and cannot stop analysing their own feelings long enough to act well. Herzog writes letter after letter he never sends. Henderson drums the world with his enormous need. These characters are **incorruptible** only in their capacity to see their own failures with merciless clarity.

Bellow understood that the American intellectual comedy — the immigrant who mastered the culture and then found the culture insufficient — was one of the defining stories of the twentieth century. He told it with density, speed, and the rhythms of a mind that never stopped working.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1976. The committee cited his "human understanding and subtle analysis of contemporary culture."

## John Galsworthy (1867–1933), United Kingdom

John Galsworthy was born into the English upper middle class and spent his life describing it from the inside with the precision and the slight nausea of someone who cannot entirely leave. *The Forsyte Saga* follows a wealthy London family across three generations, charting the long, slow collapse of a world that was always more anxious than it appeared.

The Forsytes do not do wrong in dramatic ways. They do wrong in the small, accumulated ways that respectable people do wrong: they prioritise property over feeling, inheritance over love, the appearance of honour over its substance. Galsworthy's irony is **oblique** — he does not mock his characters. He simply shows you what they cost each other, and leaves the judgement to you.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1932. The committee praised "his distinguished art of narration which takes its highest form in *The Forsyte Saga*." He died before the prize ceremony.

## Alice Munro (1931–2024), Canada

Alice Munro grew up in rural Ontario, the daughter of a fox and mink farmer. She married young, raised three children, and published her first short story collection at thirty-seven. She spent fifty years writing stories set in small Ontario towns and the lives of women who grew up in them, and in doing so she became the most formally innovative short story writer in the English language.

Her stories do not obey chronology. Time folds. A character will be introduced as an old woman and then, in the middle of a paragraph, become a child again — not as memory but as the present tense of the narrative. She understands that a life is not a line. It is a series of **oblique** approaches to things that cannot be looked at directly.

The women in her stories carry, with **impassive** precision, the weight of choices made and not made, lives lived beside the lives they might have had. Nothing is resolved. That is the truth she is after.

She received the Nobel Prize in 2013, described as "master of the contemporary short story." She died in 2024.
