## Winston Churchill (1874–1965), United Kingdom

Winston Churchill saved his country with sentences. This is not a metaphor. In the summer of 1940, when France had fallen and the German army stood at the Channel and most of the British government believed that a negotiated peace was the only rational option, Churchill stood at a microphone and said: *"We shall fight on the beaches."* The country heard him and changed its mind about what was possible.

His prose is **deliberate** architecture: Anglo-Saxon simplicity inside a formal Latinate frame, the short word beside the long, the plain statement beside the periodic sentence. He understood, as few politicians have, that rhetoric is not decoration but decision — that the sentence which cannot be doubted creates the action it describes.

He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1953, for his historical writings. The committee praised his "mastery of historical and biographical description as well as for brilliant oratory." He was the only British prime minister to win it.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), United Kingdom

Bertrand Russell was one of the most important philosophers of the twentieth century and one of its most readable prose writers — a combination so rare it constitutes a minor miracle. He believed that clarity of language was not merely a stylistic virtue but a moral one: vague prose, he argued, produces vague thought, and vague thought produces preventable suffering.

His essays move with the ease of a mind that has genuinely understood something and wants you to understand it too. He does not condescend. He does not simplify. He trusts the reader to follow an argument if it is built carefully enough, and he builds his arguments with the patience of a mathematician who knows that one skipped step undoes everything.

He was a pacifist, a campaigner for nuclear disarmament, and an atheist who wrote about religion with respect and without belief. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950 — not for philosophy, but for his writing on behalf of "humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought."

## George Bernard Shaw (1856–1950), Ireland/United Kingdom

George Bernard Shaw understood that the English class system was maintained not by law or force but by accent. If you spoke as the upper classes spoke, you were assumed to be one of them. If you did not, no amount of intelligence or talent would compensate. *Pygmalion* — his most famous play — is a proof of this argument performed on stage.

His dialogue is **incorruptible** in its sharpness: every character speaks exactly as their position in the world requires, and Shaw shows you the machinery behind the performance. His wit is not ornamental. It is surgical. He uses laughter to keep the audience watching something they would otherwise find too uncomfortable to see.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1925. He initially refused it, calling prizes "a leper's bell" used to signal that a writer had stopped being dangerous. Then he accepted the money and donated it to fund translations of Swedish literature into English.

## Bob Dylan (b. 1941), United States

Bob Dylan was born Robert Allen Zimmerman in Duluth, Minnesota, and named himself after Dylan Thomas. He wrote his first great songs at twenty. He has not stopped writing since.

His Nobel Prize in 2016 divided opinion sharply, which was appropriate: his work has divided opinion since 1965, when he plugged in an electric guitar at the Newport Folk Festival and half the audience booed. The question his prize raises — *is a song literature?* — does not have a clean answer, and that is part of why it is worth asking.

What is not in question is his command of the rhetorical traditions he drew on: the protest ballad, the biblical cadence, the **vernacular** American lyric, the **oblique** statement that means something entirely different from what it says. *Blowin' in the Wind* is a series of rhetorical questions that refuse to be answered. That refusal is the argument.

He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2016. He did not attend the ceremony.
