## W.B. Yeats (1865–1939), Ireland

William Butler Yeats was born in Dublin and grew up between Ireland and England. He did not fit well in either place. Ireland was fighting for its life. England did not take the fight seriously.

He studied the old Irish myths. He believed in ghosts. He fell in love with a woman named Maud Gonne who would not love him back. He wrote poems about all of it.

His early poems are soft and dreamlike. His later poems are hard. They have **stark** images: a gyre spinning apart, a rough beast walking toward a city, a terrible beauty. He learned that beauty could hold something **relentless** inside it without breaking.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1923. He spent part of it renovating a Norman tower in County Galway. He called it Thoor Ballylee. He wrote poems there about time and ruin.

## Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), India

Rabindranath Tagore was born in Calcutta into a family that produced poets, musicians and reformers. He wrote his first poem at eight. By thirty he had written hundreds of songs in Bengali. He ran a school in rural Bengal where children studied under trees.

In 1910 he began translating some of his own poems into English. He chose prose, not verse. He poured Bengali songs into English paragraphs and watched what survived the crossing.

His English is unlike any other English poet's. It sounds like a man speaking carefully in a language that was not built for what he needs to say. The images are **concrete** — vessels, rivers, lamps, dust — but they hold something **substantial** that the words only point toward.

He received the Nobel Prize in 1913. He was the first non-European laureate. He later returned his British knighthood in protest against the Amritsar massacre of 1919.

## Louise Glück (b. 1943), United States

Louise Glück was born in New York. She published her first book of poems at twenty-five. She has spent fifty years writing with great care and great patience. She does not publish often. Each book is **deliberate**.

Her poems are short and cold and exact. She writes about a garden the way another writer might write about a war. She writes about flowers and seasons and the dead as if they are all in the same room and only she can hear them.

She uses the word *you* the way a surgeon uses a scalpel: precisely, without warning, and aimed directly at something soft. You do not always know if she is speaking to God or to you or to herself. She does not explain.

She received the Nobel Prize in 2020. The committee called her voice "unmistakable." They were right.
