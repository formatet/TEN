title: "Seamus Heaney (1939–2013)"
publish: "true"
---
Seamus Heaney grew up with his hands in the Irish earth. His father cut turf in the boglands of County Derry; his grandfather dug before him. When Heaney chose the pen over the spade, he did not leave digging behind — he transformed it into a different kind of excavation.

His early poetry finds its **pastoral** grammar in bogs, wells, and the small archaeology of farm life. But the landscape in Heaney is never merely landscape. It holds the dead. The Iron Age bodies preserved in northern peat for two thousand years become, in his poems, his own generation's murdered — and Heaney becomes the **complicit** observer who makes **elegy** from other people's wounds.

His Nobel Prize came in 1995. The committee praised his "lyrical beauty and ethical depth." He understood those two terms as a problem, not a praise. The beauty, he knew, could not be fully innocent of the wound it named.

His Nobel Lecture (1995), "Crediting Poetry", begins in a three-room thatched farmhouse in County Derry, where a boy first heard the name *Stockholm* on the wireless. Read it, or hear him give it, at [NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1995/heaney/lecture/).
