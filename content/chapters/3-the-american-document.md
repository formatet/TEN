> *"If a writer of prose knows enough about what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them."*
> — Ernest Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon*

---

## A Note on This Chapter

You have spent two chapters learning to see. Chapter One showed you that good writing begins with the physical world — its weight, its texture, its refusal to be sentimentalised. Chapter Two showed you that an image can hold more than its surface.

Chapter Three asks a different question: what happens when you take everything away?

The three American writers in this chapter are defined by what they remove. Hemingway removes explanation, interiority, and most adjectives. Lewis removes dignity, illusion, and the comfortable distance between reader and subject. Faulkner removes, in a different way — not brevity but chronology, not silence but the opposite: the sentence that cannot end because the mind it belongs to cannot find a resting place.

These are three American documents. They describe the same country from different angles and with radically different tools. Together they show you that style is not decoration. Style is the writer's theory of what the reader needs.

## Before We Begin

Read these three sentences without context.

> *"In the late summer of that year we lived in a house in a village that looked across the river and the plain to the mountains."*

> *"Through the fence, between the curling flower spaces, I could see them hitting."*

> *"He was known, when he bothered to think about it, as a Good Fellow."*

Write: What do these three sentences expect of you? What does each one give you, and what does each one withhold?

## Part One — Hemingway

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1954. See [Gallery](/gallery/ernest-hemingway/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Hemingway learned to write as a journalist. Journalism teaches a specific discipline: say what happened, say it once, say it clearly, stop. He took that discipline into fiction and pushed it to an extreme no journalist would recognise.

His theory is called the Iceberg Principle. He stated it in *Death in the Afternoon* (1932): a writer who knows enough about their subject can omit almost everything, and the reader will feel the weight of what was omitted without being told it was there. The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. The seven-eighths below the surface hold everything up.

What this means in practice: Hemingway's characters rarely explain what they feel. They order a drink. They look out a window. They say something that has nothing to do with what they mean. The reader fills in the rest — and fills it in with something more powerful than any sentence Hemingway could have written.

### Satellite Text 1

**Source:** Ernest Hemingway, "Hills Like White Elephants", from *Men Without Women* (1927).

This story contains almost no explicit information about what is happening. A man and a woman sit at a train station in Spain. They drink. They talk. They disagree about something that is never named.

**Before reading:** The title refers to something one of the characters says. Keep that in mind.

**Read the story.** Then, before any discussion, write answers to these three questions:

1. What are the man and woman disagreeing about? (You will not find a direct answer in the text. Write your best interpretation and explain what in the text led you to it.)

2. Find three things the man says that are not about what he is actually talking about. What is he actually talking about each time?

3. Hemingway ends the story with a statement that appears to be resolution. Read it again. Is it?

**Close Reading: The Architecture of Omission**

*Step 1 — The adjective audit.*
Go through the first two pages. List every adjective. How many are there? What kind — physical descriptions, or descriptions of feelings?

*Step 2 — The dialogue as iceberg.*
Choose any four consecutive lines of dialogue. Below each line, write what the speaker is actually communicating. How much of the conversation is surface, and how much is the seven-eighths below?

*Step 3 — The landscape.*
The hills like white elephants appear once in the title and once in the story. What do they look like? Find the description. Now ask: why does Hemingway put this image here? What does the landscape do in this story that a character cannot do?

*Step 4 — The unwritten sentence.*
Identify the moment in the story where you feel most certain about what one character is feeling. Write the sentence Hemingway did not write — the sentence that states it directly. Then ask: is the story better or worse with your sentence in it?

## Part Two — Faulkner

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1949. See [Gallery](/gallery/william-faulkner/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Faulkner is Hemingway's necessary opposite. Where Hemingway removes, Faulkner accumulates. Where Hemingway builds silence, Faulkner builds pressure — sentences that spiral inward through memory, time, and the specific gravity of the American South's history.

His most radical technique is the destruction of chronological time. *The Sound and the Fury* is told partly through Benjy Compson, who has severe cognitive disabilities and experiences time non-linearly: a memory from 1910 and an event from 1928 are equally present, equally now. The sentence doesn't end because for Benjy, nothing ends.

This is not Modernist experiment for its own sake. It is a formal argument: the past doesn't pass. The South's history — slavery, defeat, the long violence of Reconstruction — is not historical for Faulkner's characters. It is present tense. The sentence that cannot end is a sentence about a wound that hasn't closed.

### Satellite Text 3

**Source:** William Faulkner, *The Sound and the Fury* (1929), opening pages (Benjy section).

**Before reading:** The narrator, Benjy, cannot understand time as sequence. Events from different years appear in the same sentence without warning. Italics indicate a shift between time periods in some editions, but not all. You may be disoriented. That is the correct experience.

**Read the opening.** Then:

1. Identify one moment where you are certain the narrative has shifted between time periods. What told you?

2. Compare the first sentence of this extract with the first sentence of *A Farewell to Arms* (Satellite Text 2). Write three specific grammatical differences. What does each difference tell you about the narrator?

3. Hemingway and Faulkner are both American, writing in the same decade, about violence and loss. Why do their sentences sound nothing alike? Write a hypothesis.

**Close Reading: The Sentence as Mind**

*Step 1:* Find the longest sentence in your extract. Count its clauses. Draw a diagram — main clause, then each subordinate clause branching off. What does the structure look like?

*Step 2:* Find the shortest sentence or sentence fragment. What does its brevity do in context?

*Step 3:* Faulkner's Nobel Prize speech (1950, available at nobelprize.org) is one of the most celebrated in the ceremony's history. He says a writer must write about "the old universal truths." Read it (3 pages). Then ask: how does a man who writes sentences that refuse to end get called a writer of universal truths?

## Part Three — Lewis

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1930. See [Gallery](/gallery/sinclair-lewis/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Lewis is the chapter's most uncomfortable writer. Hemingway respects his characters. Faulkner loves his. Lewis dissects his — with the precision and the suppressed contempt of a man who was exactly the kind of person he was writing about and never fully escaped it.

*Babbitt* (1922) is the portrait of a middle-aged real estate agent in a Midwestern American city. George F. Babbitt belongs to the right clubs, drives the right car, votes the right way, says the right things at the right dinners. He is "a Good Fellow." The novel is a precise and devastating document of a certain American emptiness.

Lewis's prose is vernacular: it sounds exactly like the people it describes. That is the technique and the cruelty. His characters indict themselves with their own vocabulary.

### Satellite Text 4

**Source:** Sinclair Lewis, *Babbitt* (1922), Chapter 1. Public domain.

**Before reading:** Lewis opens *Babbitt* with a description of a city, then a house, then a bedroom, then a man waking up. It takes four pages to get to the man. Pay attention to what Lewis is telling you with that sequence.

**Read Chapter 1.** Then:

1. Lewis describes Babbitt's alarm clock, his bathroom, his car — before he describes Babbitt's face or character. What argument is Lewis making with this order?

2. Find three sentences where Babbitt's own language reveals something Babbitt himself is unaware of. Explain the gap between what he says and what it reveals.

3. Compare Lewis's opening sentence about Babbitt with the sentences we've read from Hemingway and Faulkner. What is different about the *relationship between writer and subject*?

**Close Reading: The Vernacular as Evidence**

*Step 1:* Lewis uses Babbitt's own vocabulary — the language of advertising, boosterism, and middle-class American aspiration — as his primary critical tool. Find five words or phrases that are clearly Babbitt's vocabulary, not Lewis's. What does each one reveal?

*Step 2:* Irony is present throughout this chapter but never stated. Find one moment of irony — identify what is said, what is meant, and what the gap reveals.

*Step 3:* Lewis, Hemingway, and Faulkner are all writing about American men in the early twentieth century. What do these three men have in common? What is the writer's diagnosis in each case?

## The Writing Task

This is a Year One writing task focused on the central skill of Chapter Three: productive omission.

**Step 1 — The original scene:**
Choose a scene you know well: from a film, a novel, or your own experience. It should be a scene with emotional weight — a moment of loss, anger, love, or fear. Write it in 150 words as you would normally write it: with interiority, with adjectives describing feelings, with explanation.

**Step 2 — The Hemingway rewrite:**
Rewrite the same scene in 100 words or fewer. Remove:
- All adjectives that describe feelings (*sad*, *frightened*, *angry*)
- All interiority (*she thought*, *he felt*, *she knew*)
- All explanation of what the scene means

Keep only: what can be seen, heard, touched. Actions and dialogue. The physical world.

**Step 3 — The comparison:**
Read both versions to a partner. Ask them: what is the difference? Which version do they trust more — and why?

Write one sentence explaining what you lost and one sentence explaining what you gained.

## The Big Question

*Hemingway. Faulkner. Lewis.*

Three American writers. Three different answers to the same problem: how do you write about a country that cannot look at itself honestly?

- Hemingway leaves it out.
- Faulkner circles it without stopping.
- Lewis makes the country describe itself.

**For discussion:**

Which of these three strategies is *kinder* to the reader — and which is *more honest*?

Are kindness and honesty the same thing in literature?

Write one sentence before the discussion begins. Revise it at the end.
