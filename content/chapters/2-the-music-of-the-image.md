## A Note on This Chapter

In Year One, you learned that good writing begins with observation: seeing the world as it actually is, without sentimentality or decoration. Chapter One showed you what that means in narrative prose.

Chapter Two asks something harder: what happens to language when it has to hold something that cannot be explained?

That is what a poem is for. Not to *say* what the poet feels. To *show* something so precisely that the feeling arrives in the reader without being announced.

The three poets in this chapter lived on different continents and wrote in different centuries. Yeats wrote from the edge of empire, watching Ireland try to become itself. Tagore wrote from Bengal, translating his own Bengali poems into English for a world that barely knew his country existed. Glück writes from the interior of a single life, finding in a garden the architecture of mortality.

None of them explains. All of them show.

At the end of this chapter, you will write a short poem of your own. It will contain no adjectives that describe feelings. It will contain no abstractions. It will contain only things — and the things will do all the work.

Read these three lines. Do not look up who wrote them.

## Part One – W.B. Yeats (1865–1939)

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1923. See [Gallery](/gallery/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Yeats was born in Dublin in 1865, the son of a painter. He spent his life between Ireland and England, between ancient myth and modern politics, between mysticism and the hard facts of history.

He is the poet who watched the Easter Rising of 1916 — the Irish republican rebellion against British rule — from a distance, and then had to decide what to make of it in words. The phrase he arrived at — *"a terrible beauty is born"* — is one of the most compressed political statements in English poetry. It holds two things that should contradict each other, and refuses to resolve them.

His early poetry is dreamlike and decorative. His later poetry — the work that matters most for this course — is hard, exact, and disturbing.

### Satellite Text 1

**Source:** W.B. Yeats, *Michael Robartes and the Dancer* (1921). Public domain.

This poem was written in 1919, immediately after the First World War and the Russian Revolution. Yeats believed history moved in two-thousand-year spirals — *gyres* — and that one age was ending and another, darker one beginning.

**Read the poem.** Then answer in writing before any discussion:

1. The first stanza describes a world where the centre is lost and things are falling apart. Find three specific images Yeats uses — not abstract statements, but *images* — that create this feeling. Write them down.

2. The creature in the second stanza is called "the rough beast." Yeats does not explain what it represents. Why not? What would be lost if he explained it?

3. The last line of the poem is: *"And what rough beast, its hour come round at last, / Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?"* This is a question. Why does the poem end with a question? What would change if Yeats had answered it?

**Close Reading: The Image as Argument**

*Step 1 – Concrete vs. abstract.*
Make two lists from the poem: (a) concrete nouns and verbs — things you can touch, see, or hear; (b) abstract nouns — words for ideas, qualities, or states.
Which list is longer? Which carries more power?

*Step 2 – The falcon and the falconer.*
The poem opens with a falcon that "cannot hear the falconer." This is an image — not a statement. Rewrite the same idea as a direct statement: what is Yeats actually saying about the world?
Now ask: which version is stronger? What does the image give that the statement takes away?

*Step 3 – What the poem does not tell us.*
The "rough beast" is one of the most famous images in twentieth-century poetry. It has been interpreted as communism, fascism, the Antichrist, and many other things. Yeats does not specify.
Write two sentences: (a) what is lost by the ambiguity, and (b) what is gained.

### Satellite Text 2

**Source:** W.B. Yeats, *Michael Robartes and the Dancer* (1921). Public domain.

**Before reading:** The Easter Rising was a failed rebellion. Most of the leaders were executed by the British. Yeats had known some of them personally and had not thought highly of them. After the executions, he was forced to reconsider.

**Read the final stanza**, which ends with the phrase *"a terrible beauty is born."*

**One question only:** How does Yeats hold "terrible" and "beauty" together in the same sentence without it collapsing? What makes the paradox work?

Write one paragraph. This is a hard question for Year One. It is supposed to be hard.

## Part Two — Tagore

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1913. See [Gallery](/gallery/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Tagore was born in Calcutta in 1861, into a wealthy Bengali family that was already famous for its intellectuals, reformers, and poets. He had already written thousands of poems and songs in Bengali when, in 1910, he began translating some of them into English prose-poems — a form he invented for the purpose. The result was *Gitanjali* (*Song Offerings*), published in English in 1912.

He won the Nobel Prize in 1913. He was the first non-European laureate. The Nobel Committee cited his "deeply sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse."

Tagore's English is unusual. It does not sound like an English poet trying to write spiritual verse. It sounds like someone trying to hold, in a language that was not designed for the purpose, something that already existed perfectly in another language. That tension is part of what makes it beautiful.

**Note on language:** Tagore wrote in Bengali. The English texts we read are his own translations — not translations of his poetry into English poetry, but translations of his poetry into English prose. That choice tells us something. Keep it in mind.

### Satellite Text 3

**Source:** Rabindranath Tagore, *Gitanjali* (1912). Public domain.

**Read verses 1 and 35 first.** Then:

1. Verse 1 opens: *"Thou hast made me endless, such is thy pleasure. This frail vessel thou emptiest again and again, and fillest it ever with fresh life."*
The vessel — the cup — is an image. What does it hold? What does Tagore avoid saying directly by using the image instead?

2. Verse 35 includes the line: *"Let only that little be left of me whereby I may name thee my all."*
This is a prayer for reduction — to become less in order to give more. Rewrite this as a direct statement. What is lost in the translation from image to statement?

3. Tagore wrote these as Bengali songs, then rewrote them in English prose. Why prose and not English verse? Write a hypothesis in two sentences.

**Close Reading: The Grammar of Offering**

Tagore's sentences often follow a pattern: *gift → giver → what is given back*. This is the grammar of devotion — giving and receiving are not separate acts but a single circulation.

Find one sentence in the verses you have read that follows this pattern. Copy it. Then write a sentence of your own that uses the same grammatical movement — but about something from your own experience, not about God.

## Part Three – Louise Glück (b. 1943)

*Nobel Prize in Literature 2020. See [Gallery](/gallery/) for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Louise Glück was born in New York in 1943. She has published twelve collections of poetry over fifty years. Her work is sparse, precise, and uncomfortable in a way that is hard to name: she writes about small things — a garden, a family, a season — and by the end of the poem the small thing has become the whole of existence.

She uses the second-person *you* in ways that collapse the distance between the poem and the reader. When Glück says *"you"*, it is not entirely clear who she is addressing — the reader, God, the dead, or herself. That ambiguity is structural, not accidental.

She received the Nobel Prize in 2020. The committee described her voice as "unmistakable." It is. Within two lines of a Glück poem, you know whose language you are in.

### Satellite Text 4

**Source:** Louise Glück, *The Wild Iris* (1992), Ecco/HarperCollins.

**Before reading:** The poem is spoken by an iris — a flower — in the first person. It is not a metaphor. The flower is the speaker. Glück does not explain or apologize for this.

**Read the poem** once silently, then once aloud.

1. The poem's first line begins: *"At the end of my suffering / there was a door."* The flower has suffered. The flower found a door. Why is this not absurd? What makes it convincing?

2. Find the moment in the poem where the perspective shifts — where "I" (the flower) begins to speak directly to "you" (the reader, or someone). What changes when that shift happens?

3. The poem is partly about resurrection — coming back after apparent death. But it never uses the words *death*, *rebirth*, *resurrection*, or *God*. How does Glück achieve the subject without naming it?

**Close Reading: The Second-Person Address**

Glück's *you* is one of the most studied features of her work. It creates intimacy and also a slight terror — the reader is pulled in before they have agreed to be addressed.

*Step 1:* Find one line where *you* could mean the reader. Find one where it could mean someone else (God, the dead, a specific person). They may be the same line.

*Step 2:* Rewrite one of those lines replacing *you* with a specific noun (*God*, *my mother*, *the reader*). What is lost?

*Step 3:* Write two lines of your own using *you* in a way that is deliberately ambiguous about who you are addressing.

## Eliot – An Introduction

T.S. Eliot (1888–1965) received the Nobel Prize in 1948. He appears properly in Chapter Seven as the author of *The Waste Land*. He is introduced here because Chapter Two — the image, the line, the poem's refusal to explain — is impossible to understand without knowing what Modernism did to English poetry.

Before Eliot (and Yeats, and Pound, who shaped them both), English poetry was expected to be decorative, melodic, and reassuring. After Eliot, a poem could be fragmentary, allusive, and deliberately difficult — and this would be understood as honesty, not failure.

**One passage only.** The opening six lines of *The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock* (1915):

> *Let us go then, you and I,*
> *When the evening is spread out against the sky*
> *Like a patient etherised upon a table;*

Eliot compares the evening sky to a patient under anaesthesia. This is not beautiful in any conventional sense. It is exact. It is wrong and right at the same time.

**One question:** What does the evening sky look like when compared to this? Can you see it? If you can, Eliot has done his work.

## The Writing Task

This is a Year One writing task. It is simple to explain and difficult to do well.

**The task:**

Write a poem of eight to twelve lines. The poem must contain:

- At least four concrete nouns (things you can touch or see)
- At least two strong verbs (verbs that show action, not state)
- No adjectives that describe *feelings* (not *beautiful*, *sad*, *happy*, *wonderful*)
- No abstractions in the first six lines (*love*, *truth*, *meaning*, *hope* are not permitted until you have earned them with a concrete image)

The subject may be anything from your life: a person, a place, a sound, a moment you cannot forget.

**The rule is simple:** *Show me the thing. Do not tell me what it means.*

**Before writing:**
Look back at the three opening lines from the start of this chapter. Notice how each one works with a concrete image to hold something large. You are going to do the same.

**After writing:**
Exchange with a partner. Ask each other: *Where does the poem tell me what to feel? Find those places and remove them. Now: does the poem still work?*

## The Big Question

*Yeats. Tagore. Glück.*

Three poets. Three continents. Three centuries. One question each:

- Yeats: *When does a poem become a political act?*
- Tagore: *Can you translate a feeling, or only the form of a feeling?*
- Glück: *What can a flower say that a person cannot?*

**For class discussion:**

These questions look different. But they may all be asking the same thing.

What is that thing?

Write one sentence — before the discussion begins — that is your answer. You will have the chance to change it at the end of the lesson.
