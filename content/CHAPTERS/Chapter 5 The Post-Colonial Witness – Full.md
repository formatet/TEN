## A Note on This Chapter

In Chapter Four you studied language as power: how a sentence is built to move a listener, to construct a world, to make one choice feel inevitable. That was the view from inside power.

Chapter Five asks what happens to language when you are on the other side of it.

English arrived in Nigeria, South Africa, the Caribbean, Zanzibar, and Australia in the same ships as soldiers and administrators. It was not offered as a gift. It came with the implicit condition that it would replace — or at least outrank — what was already there. The writers in this chapter all inherited that condition. None of them refused the language. All of them changed what it could say.

This is not a chapter about victimhood. It is a chapter about a more complicated and more interesting process: what happens when a language that was used against you becomes, in your hands, one of your sharpest tools.

The five writers here represent five different positions: the Nigerian dramatist who dismantles European humanism with its own theatrical weapons; the white South African who turned her privilege into a witness rather than a shelter; the Caribbean poet who looked at Homer and said *mine*; the Zanzibari exile who writes about loss in the language of his coloniser; and the Australian whose landscape refuses to be what a European mind needs it to be.

Together they answer a question that will follow you for the rest of this course: *Who owns a language?*

## Part One – Wole Soyinka (b. 1934)

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1986. See The Gallery for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Wole Soyinka was the first African writer to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature. He was born in Abeokuta, Nigeria, educated partly in Leeds, and has spent his adult life moving between theatre, prison, and exile — sometimes in rapid succession.

His most famous statement about African literature is the one quoted above: *"A tiger does not proclaim its tigritude, it pounces."* He said this in 1962, arguing against the *Négritude* movement — the French-language movement that celebrated African cultural identity. Soyinka's position was not that African identity was unimportant. It was that a tiger doesn't need to announce itself. It acts.

His work is built on Yoruba theatrical tradition — not adapted to European forms but used to dismantle them. When colonialism claimed to bring civilisation, Soyinka turned the claim inside out by writing in the coloniser's language about the coloniser's failures, using the coloniser's own genre (drama) against the coloniser's own self-image.

### Satellite Text 1

**Source:** Wole Soyinka, Nobel Lecture, 8 December 1986. Available free at nobelprize.org.

Soyinka's Nobel lecture is explicitly political. He uses the platform to speak about apartheid South Africa and Nelson Mandela's imprisonment. It is one of the most direct political acts in the Nobel lecture's history.

**Before reading:** The lecture's title is "This Past Must Address Its Present." Notice the unusual grammar: the past addresses the present, not the other way around. Keep that inversion in mind.

**Read the selected passages.** Then:

1. Soyinka argues that the Nobel Prize has a "symbolic weight" that creates obligations. What obligations? Do you agree that receiving a prize creates obligations?

2. Find one sentence where Soyinka uses the language of European humanism (ideas of civilisation, progress, human dignity) against the European powers that claim to represent those values. How does this rhetorical move work?

3. Return to Chapter Four: Churchill, Russell, Shaw, Dylan. Where does Soyinka's rhetoric sit in relation to theirs? Is he closer to Churchill (mobilising), Russell (dismantling assumptions), or Shaw (using the language of power against itself)?

**Close Reading: The Political Sentence**

*Step 1:* Soyinka builds his argument by accumulating specific historical evidence rather than abstract principles. Find a passage of three or four sentences that exemplifies this method. What is the effect of the specific over the general?

*Step 2:* Find a sentence where the grammar itself enacts the argument — where how the sentence is built is part of what it means.

*Step 3:* Soyinka is writing in English about events that affected people who mostly did not speak English. Who is the intended audience for this lecture? And who is the actual audience? Are they the same?

## Part Two — Gordimer

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1991. See The Gallery for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Nadine Gordimer grew up white in South Africa. This is the essential fact. Everything else — her Nobel Prize, her banned books, her lifelong opposition to apartheid — proceeds from the question of what a white woman in South Africa does with the accident of her birth.

Her answer was: she looked. She kept looking even when looking was uncomfortable. She wrote about white South Africans not as heroes of resistance but as people navigating the ordinary compromises that a system of organised racial injustice requires of everyone who benefits from it — which is to say, everyone who is white.

Her moral position was not comfortable. She was criticised by the apartheid government for her opposition, and by some Black South African activists for speaking about their experience as an outsider. She accepted both criticisms as costs of the only position available to her: the one she was in.

### Satellite Text 2

**Source:** Nadine Gordimer, from *Jump and Other Stories* (1991) or *Selected Stories* (1975).

**Before reading:** Gordimer rarely tells you directly what she thinks about her characters. She shows you what they do, what they say, what they notice and what they don't notice. The judgement is in the gap between what a character believes about themselves and what their behaviour reveals.

**Read the extract.** Then:

1. Identify the central character's moral blind spot — the thing they cannot see about themselves. Find a moment in the text where this is visible to the reader but not to the character.

2. Gordimer uses the term "complicit" (see your archive) in her own critical writing about white South Africans. Find a moment in the text where a character is complicit in a system they might sincerely believe they oppose. What makes the complicity visible?

3. Compare Gordimer's narrative position with Soyinka's. Both are witnesses. But they are witnessing from different positions. Describe the difference — and what each position can and cannot see.

## Part Three — Walcott

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1992. See The Gallery for full biographical entry.*

### Context for This Chapter

Derek Walcott grew up on Saint Lucia in the Caribbean, the child of a civil servant father who died when Walcott was one year old and a schoolteacher mother who read Shakespeare aloud to her classes. He learned English literature in a British-designed colonial school system and emerged from it having decided that Homer belonged to him as much as to anyone in Oxford.

His epic poem *Omeros* (1990) retells the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* with Saint Lucian fishermen as its heroes, the Caribbean Sea as its wine-dark water, and the history of the Middle Passage as its wound. He was not using Homer as a reference or an ironic frame. He was claiming him — taking one of the foundational texts of Western civilisation and saying: *this is mine too, and through it, so is everything it carries.*

His is one of the most powerful answers to the question of what you do with a coloniser's language: you use it to claim the coloniser's entire inheritance.

### Satellite Text 3

**Source:** Derek Walcott, *In a Green Night* (1962).

This poem was written in response to the Mau Mau Uprising in Kenya (1952–1960), during which Kenyan freedom fighters and British colonial forces committed atrocities against each other. Walcott, as a Caribbean man of mixed African and European descent writing in English, found himself unable to take a simple side.

**Before reading:** The poem's last lines are: *"I who am poisoned with the blood of both, / Where shall I turn, divided to the vein?"* Keep that ending in mind as you read.

**Read the poem.** Then:

1. The poem is structured as a series of arguments the speaker makes and then undermines. Find one moment where the speaker takes a position, and then the next lines complicate or reverse it. What is Walcott doing with his own argument?

2. "A Far Cry from Africa" is a phrase that can be read two ways. What are they? Which reading do you think Walcott intended — or did he intend both?

3. Compare Walcott's position with Gordimer's. Both writers are caught between two sides of a colonial conflict. But their positions are different. Describe the difference — what makes each position uniquely theirs?

**Close Reading: The Divided Line**

*Step 1:* Walcott uses images of both beauty and violence throughout the poem, sometimes in the same line. Find one example. What is the effect of holding them together rather than separating them?

*Step 2:* The poem ends with a question. Walcott does not answer it. Compare this with Yeats's ending in "The Second Coming" (Chapter 2). Why do certain poets end with unanswered questions? What does the refusal to answer do?

*Step 3:* Return to your archive: COLONIAL, TESTIMONY, DIASPORA, ELEGIAC. Choose two and show how each one illuminates a different aspect of this poem.

## Gurnah and White: Two Further Witnesses

These two writers are given shorter treatment here. They extend the chapter's reach without requiring full close-reading apparatus — but they should not be skipped.

### Abdulrazak Gurnah (b. 1948)

*Nobel Prize in Literature 2021. See The Gallery for full biographical entry.*

Gurnah was born in Zanzibar and arrived in England as a refugee in the 1960s. He has spent his adult life in England, writing in English about what it means to carry a world that no longer fully exists.

His Nobel Prize surprised many readers who had never heard of him. Gurnah said in response that he had always known there were readers who hadn't read him. He kept writing anyway.

**Reading:** *By the Sea* (2001), opening pages — available via library.

**One question only:** Gurnah's narrator speaks English with the precision of someone who learned it formally. How does this formal precision carry — or conceal — the narrator's history? Find one sentence where the gap between the language and the speaker is visible.

---

### Patrick White

*Nobel Prize in Literature 1973. See The Gallery for full biographical entry.*

White is the chapter's uncomfortable counterpoint: not the colonised voice reclaiming the language, but the coloniser's descendant discovering that the landscape of his inheritance does not recognise him.

**Reading:** Excerpt from *Voss* (1957) — the novel's opening chapters.

**One question only:** White describes the Australian landscape through the eyes of a European explorer who cannot see what it actually is. Find a moment where the landscape resists description — where the European vocabulary is inadequate. What does that inadequacy reveal?

## The Writing Task

This is a Year Two writing task. It requires you to take a position and hold it under pressure.

**The question:**

*Can you write honestly about an experience that is not your own?*

Gordimer, Soyinka, Walcott, and Gurnah all write about experiences of colonial violence, exile, and dispossession — but from different positions relative to those experiences. Gordimer is inside the system that perpetuates the violence. Soyinka speaks from the position of the colonised. Walcott is divided. Gurnah writes from exile.

**Your task:** Write 400–500 words arguing for a position on this question, using at least two of the writers from this chapter as evidence. Your essay must:
- State a clear position in the opening paragraph
- Use specific textual evidence (quote and analyse — do not only summarise)
- Address at least one counterargument
- End with a conclusion that acknowledges complexity without abandoning your position

**The extra difficulty:** Your position on the question applies to you too. What is your position relative to the experiences you are writing about?
