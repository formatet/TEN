## The Rhetoric of Power: Churchill, Russell, Shaw, Dylan

*Part of [The Etymological Archive](/archive/) | Chapter thread: language as an instrument of power — and as resistance to power*

---

The words in this chapter are not merely words — they are tools people have used to change worlds and defend them. The etymology shows that every concept carries a historical battlefield inside it.

---

## The Words

**RHETORIC**
- Origin: Greek *rhētorikē* — the art of speaking; from *rhētor* = speaker, from *rhē-* = to flow, to speak
- Weight: Rhetoric is not manipulation — it is *the technique* of persuasion. Aristotle defined it as the capacity to see, in any given situation, the available means of persuasion. Today the word is often used dismissively ("mere rhetoric") but that is a late, defensive move: the person who calls another's argument "mere rhetoric" has usually lost the argument.
- Avoid instead: *propaganda, speechmaking*
- Write: Churchill and Dylan are both rhetoricians — one in parliament, one in songs. Write a sentence where the word RHETORIC can be applied to Dylan without sounding dismissive.
- Connection: Russell understood that rhetoric can be an instrument of truth — not only of power. That is the distinction the chapter examines.

**INEVITABLE**
- Origin: Latin *inevitabilis* — impossible to avoid; *in-* (not) + *evitare* (to avoid) — from *ex-* (out) + *vitare* (to keep away from, to shun)
- Weight: The inevitable is not merely what happens — it is what is *constructed* as inevitable. Political rhetoric makes the improbable unavoidable and the possible impossible. "We shall fight on the beaches" was a choice; Churchill's rhetorical genius was to make it sound as though it were not.
- Avoid instead: *certain, unavoidable*
- Exercise: Choose a political statement from the past day. Identify: is something being constructed as inevitable that is actually a choice? How?
- Cross-reference: Returns in [Chapter 5](/archive/chapter-5/) (colonialism's "inevitability") and [Chapter 6](/archive/chapter-6/) (irony's mechanics when the inevitable finally arrives)

**DELIBERATE**
- Origin: Latin *deliberare* — to weigh carefully; *de-* (fully) + *librare* (to weigh, balance) — from *libra* = scale, balance-weight
- Weight: Deliberate = fully considered, every word weighed. Shaw's dialogue is deliberate: every line is constructed to reveal exactly the amount of class privilege required. That is not an accident. Russell's prose is deliberate: every inserted clause is necessary, none is decoration.
- Avoid instead: *careful, intentional*
- Write: Rewrite one of your own sentences until it is deliberate in Russell's sense. How many words did you remove?
